# Narrative mode

General prose that makes an argument: memos, essays, articles, blog posts, reports, explainers, briefs, cover notes, and long emails. The goal is a coherent argument whose paragraphs move the reader forward, each one earning the next.

## Thesis

Write the thesis as one complete sentence that a reader could disagree with: "Firms that price e-discovery per gigabyte will lose the mid-market to per-matter pricing within two years." A topic ("e-discovery pricing") is not a thesis. If the sources do not support a thesis, say so and propose the narrowest one they do support.

For a document Seth reads or sends, the thesis is also the opening paragraph under `style` rule 20.

## Structure

Choose the structure from the reader's job, not from habit.

| Reader's job | Structure |
| :- | :- |
| Decide or approve | Answer first: conclusion, then the three or four reasons, each with evidence, then risks and what it gives up, then the ask. |
| Understand a problem | Problem, why it happens, what it costs, options, recommendation. |
| Follow an argument they may resist | Shared ground, the tension, the evidence in sequence, the claim, the objection and its answer, the implication. |
| Learn how something works | The one-sentence model first, then each part in the order the reader needs it, with one worked example. |
| Read for interest (essay, post) | A specific hook, the stakes, the argument developed through examples, a resolution that adds something. |

Group supporting points so they do not overlap and together cover the claim. Order them by importance for a decider and by dependency for a learner. Put completeness (method, full data, edge cases) in an appendix, not in the main line.

## Paragraph progression

- One topic per paragraph. The first sentence states it; the rest develops it.
- Each paragraph answers the question the previous one raised. If two adjacent paragraphs could swap without loss, the order is not doing work; reconsider it.
- Start sentences with what the reader already has and end them with what is new. The stress position at the end of a sentence is where the important word goes.
- Keep related words together: subject near verb, modifier next to what it modifies.
- Express parallel ideas in parallel form.
- Vary paragraph length with the content, not by rule. A one-sentence paragraph is for a turn in the argument, not for emphasis on a point already made.

## Examples and evidence

- Every abstract claim that matters gets one concrete instance: a case, a number, a quotation, a scenario with real names and real figures from the sources.
- One well-developed example beats three thin ones. Use three only when the meaning has three parts.
- Attribute every quotation and number. If a source is unnamed in the material, say so; do not upgrade "a colleague said" into "experts agree."
- Mark inference and opinion when the claim goes past what the sources show (`style` rule 25).
- Never invent an example and present it as fact. A hypothetical is fine when it is labeled as one ("Suppose a firm bills 400 hours...").

## Transitions

A transition states the logical relationship between two ideas: cause, contrast, consequence, example, concession, or sequence. Choose the word for the relationship ("because", "but", "so", "for example", "although", "then"), or let the topic sentence carry it by naming what it follows from.

- Do not use a transition word that names no real relationship ("Additionally", "Moreover", "Furthermore" stacked at paragraph starts).
- If a transition is hard to write, the paragraphs may not be connected. Fix the order or add the missing step in the argument.
- Section headings are transitions too: name what the section holds, not a teaser.

## Openings and endings

**Openings.** Start with the claim, the problem, or a specific fact. Do not open with background the reader already has, a definition from the dictionary, or a sweeping statement about the world. For a reply in a thread, lead with the answer; the reader already has the context.

**Endings.** End on the last thing that adds something: the implication, the decision needed, the next step, or the open question. Do not restate the argument, append a moral, or send the reader off with a slogan. If the final paragraph could be deleted with no loss, delete it.

## Sentences

Sentence-level rules (punctuation, banned words, hedging, lists versus prose) come from `style` and are not repeated here. Composition principles adapted from Strunk that `style` does not cover:

- Use the active voice when it makes the actor clear.
- Put statements in positive form: "forgot" rather than "did not remember."
- Prefer the definite, specific, and concrete to the general and abstract.
- Omit needless words: "the fact that", "in order to", "it is important to note that."
- Avoid a run of loose sentences joined by "and" or "which"; vary the structure.
- Keep to one tense in a summary.

## The outline read

Before delivering a new draft or a substantial revision, extract the first sentence of every paragraph and read them as a list. They should make the argument on their own, in order. If they do not, fix the structure before polishing sentences.

## Output

1. The draft, with headings when it has four or more sections.
2. A short note: the thesis sentence, the structure chosen and why, assumptions, and each `[NEED: ...]` gap.

For a narrow edit: the changed passage and one line of reason.
