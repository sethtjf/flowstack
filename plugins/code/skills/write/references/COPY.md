# Copy mode

Website copy: a homepage, landing page, pricing page, feature page, about page, or one unit of one (hero, headline, section, CTA). A page is one argument made to one reader who arrived with a question and a doubt. The copy's job is to answer the question, retire the doubt, and name the next step.

## Position before you write

Answer these from the product context, the existing site, and the brief. Mark each as sourced or inferred.

| Question | Why it matters |
| :- | :- |
| Who is the one reader for this page, and how did they arrive (ad, search, referral, email)? | Traffic source sets what they already know and which promise brought them. |
| What were they using before, and what is wrong with it in their words? | The alternative is the real competitor, often a spreadsheet, an agency, or doing nothing. |
| What does this product do that the alternative cannot? | The differentiator. If there is none in the sources, flag `[NEED: differentiator]` rather than writing around it. |
| What is the one action the page asks for? | Every section either moves the reader toward it or gets cut. |
| What proof exists? | Sets which claims the page can make. |

Positioning is a sentence before it is a page: "For <reader> who <situation>, <product> <does the specific thing>, unlike <alternative>, which <falls short in a specific way>." If this sentence cannot be written from the sources, the page cannot be either; say what is missing.

## Build the page argument

Write the page as a sequence of claims before writing any copy. Each section makes one claim and answers the question the previous section raised.

1. **Hero.** The headline states the outcome or the change for this reader. The subhead says how, in one or two sentences, with the mechanism or the differentiator. The primary CTA follows.
2. **Problem.** The reader's current situation and its cost, in their language. Short. Skip it when the reader already knows the problem well (bottom-of-funnel pages).
3. **How it works.** Three or four concrete steps. This is where complexity gets retired.
4. **Benefits.** Three to five, each a feature tied to what it means for the reader ("which means...").
5. **Proof.** Placed next to the claim it supports, not quarantined in a logo strip.
6. **Objections.** The reasons this reader would hesitate, answered (pricing, switching cost, security, "will this work for my case").
7. **Final CTA.** Restate the outcome and repeat the action, with the risk reducer that is actually true ("No card required" only if no card is required).

Read the section claims in order, without the copy. If the argument does not hold as a list of claims, no wording will save it. Fix the order first.

Not every page needs every section. A landing page from a specific ad makes one argument for one action. A homepage serves several intents but still leads with the broadest true value proposition and gives each intent a clear path. Do not add sections the user did not ask for when revising.

## Headlines

- Lead with what changes for the reader. A specific outcome beats a category label; a category label beats a slogan.
- Make it pass the copy gate below on its own.
- Match the promise that brought the reader (the ad, the search term, the email) closely enough that they know they landed in the right place.
- "Now you can <do the thing>" is a useful test: if the headline cannot be restated that way, it may describe the product rather than the change.
- Give two or three alternatives when drafting a new headline, each with a one-line rationale. For a narrow headline edit, give the edit plus at most two alternatives, and change nothing else.
- Use sentence case unless the brand guide says otherwise.

Formula starting points, to fill with specifics, never to ship as templates: "<Outcome> without <pain>", "<Product type> for <audience>", "Turn <input> into <outcome>", "<Outcome> in <real timeframe>".

## Proof

Proof is any evidence a skeptical reader could check: a named customer with a specific result, a number with its source and period, a third-party review score, a certification, a public benchmark, a guarantee in the terms of service, a screenshot of the real product.

- Only use proof that exists in the sources. Never invent a testimonial, customer name, logo, statistic, or result, and never "illustrate" with a placeholder that could ship.
- Specific beats large. "Cut month-end close from 9 days to 4 at Acme Logistics" beats "Trusted by thousands."
- Place proof next to the claim it supports.
- When a claim lacks proof, write the supportable version and flag the gap with what would unlock the stronger claim: `[NEED: customer quote with a measured result, e.g. from the Q3 case-study interviews]`.
- Round numbers that feel made up ("10x", "thousands") need a source or need to go.
- Claims about competitors, rankings ("the leading"), security, compliance, or legal outcomes need a source the user can point to. In legal technology, do not imply legal advice, guaranteed outcomes, or bar-regulated claims without the user's confirmation.

## Objections

List every reason the target reader might hesitate, from the sources (sales call notes, support tickets, reviews, FAQ) or inferred and marked as inferred. For each, decide where the page answers it: in the claim itself, in a proof point, in an FAQ entry, or near the CTA. An objection raised but not answered is worse than one not raised.

Common ones: "Is this for someone like me?", "What does switching cost?", "Is my data safe?", "What does it really cost?", "What if it does not work?", "Why not keep doing what I do now?"

## Calls to action

- Name what the reader gets or does: "Start a 14-day trial", "See pricing for my team", "Book a 20-minute demo". Avoid "Submit", "Learn more", and "Get started" unless the brand insists.
- One primary action per page. Secondary actions are visually and verbally secondary.
- Say what happens next when it is not obvious ("We email you a sandbox link within a minute"), but only if it is true.
- Put the true risk reducers next to the CTA: free trial terms, cancellation terms, "No card required."

## The copy gate

Run every line you write or keep through four tests.

| Test | Question | Fails when |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Specificity | Can the reader picture it? | It is an abstraction ("streamline your workflow") with no scene, object, number, or step. |
| Supportability | Could it be shown true or false, and do the sources support it? | It is decoration ("best-in-class"), or the proof is missing. |
| Differentiation | Could a competitor publish this exact sentence? | Yes. Rewrite from the differentiator or flag `[NEED: differentiator]`. |
| Next action | Does the reader know what to do next, and is the page moving them toward it? | A section ends without leading anywhere, or the CTA is buried, vague, or competes with another. |

A line that passes all four stays. A line that fails one gets sharpened. A line that fails two or more is rewritten from the facts or replaced by a flagged gap. Apply the gate to headlines, subheads, section heads, and CTAs first; they carry most of the weight.

Keep most lines plain and declarative. A page can afford one or two lines that try to land hard, usually the headline and one section. When every line performs, none does.

Before delivering, run the leave-it-alone check from [EDITING.md](EDITING.md#leave-it-alone): revert any change you cannot give a reason for.

## Page types

| Page | Lead with | Watch for |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Homepage | The broadest true value proposition, then paths for each visitor intent | Becoming generic to serve everyone |
| Landing page | The promise from the traffic source | A second CTA, navigation that leaks the reader away |
| Pricing | Which plan fits which reader | Hidden costs, unanswered "which is right for me?" |
| Feature page | Feature, then what it means, then the outcome | A spec sheet with no reader in it |
| About | Why the company exists, tied to what the reader gets | A founder biography with no CTA |

## Output

For a new page or substantial revision:

1. The page copy, organized by section (headline, subhead, CTA, then each section's heading and body, then the final CTA). Include page title and meta description when the page is new.
2. Headline and CTA alternatives, two or three each, one line of rationale each.
3. A short note: positioning sentence, assumptions, each `[NEED: ...]` gap, and which objections the page does not yet answer.

For a narrow edit: the changed line, at most two alternatives, and one line of reason.
